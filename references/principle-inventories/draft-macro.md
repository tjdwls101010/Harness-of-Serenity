## REGIME → AGGRESSION

### Regime is the aggression dial, not a destination
- **What:** Read the pipeline's `regime` + `risk_level` into a *posture* — how aggressive the hunt, how loud the go. Risk-On → lean aggressive on conviction; Transition → raise the bar. The moment a question is macro *and more*, run the regime read first and let every downstream name inherit the setting. Don't re-derive the gauges the pipeline computes — interpret them.
- **Why:** The same name is a buy in one regime and a pass in another; the regime is the multiplier on every conviction call, so resolving it first prevents you from sizing a thesis correctly in isolation but wrong for the tape. Treating macro as a separate answerable question (and stopping there) wastes the dial's real job — conditioning everything after it.
- **Picture:** Type A "장 어때" enters at the regime call and may stop there; "관세 때문에 뭐 사" is macro AND supply-chain AND discovery — walk the union in dependency order, regime first.

### Hyperscaler CapEx direction is THE leading indicator for the AI trade
- **What:** Of the four pillars deciding the regime — ① hyperscaler CapEx direction, ② net liquidity, ③ credit conditions, ④ VIX structure (contango vs backwardation) — CapEx direction is the load-bearing one for any AI-adjacent book: increasing = tailwind, flat/declining = reassess everything downstream. Elevated VIX *alone* is insufficient; combine the pillars.
- **Why:** The entire CapEx cascade (semis → memory → substrates → raw materials) is downstream of hyperscaler spend, so its direction is the single signal that validates or invalidates the largest cluster of theses at once — it changes forward revenue for the whole chain, not sentiment. Reading VIX in isolation gives you a fear spike with no fundamental read; you'd buy a dip the cascade is actually turning against.

### Fear-Dislocation is the best buying environment
- **What:** Fear elevated but fundamentals intact → the single best buying environment (V1). The supply-chain thesis is unchanged; the price is gifted. This is where the fear-dip discrimination (mechanism check, prove-the-math, IO-into-the-drop, kill-signal clear) earns its keep.
- **Why:** The methodology's whole edge is that the market reacts to *sentiment faster than to structure* — Fear-Dislocation is the maximal gap between price and unchanged structure, so it is by construction where the asymmetry is fattest. Naming it as the *best* environment (not just "a" buyable one) is what keeps you buying when the tape feels worst, which is exactly when the instinct is to wait.
- **Picture:** Post-liquidation snap-back: after forced selling exhausts (margin calls, redemptions), quality reverts first — wait for exhaustion, then enter.

### Crisis/Wartime is a 4th regime — structural rotation, NOT buy-the-dip
- **What:** When active conflict, a sustained geopolitical crisis, or a structural policy shift (sanctions, trade-war escalation) creates a *durable* sector rotation — not a temporary fear spike — treat it as a distinct regime the pipeline won't flag. The action is "rotate the portfolio" (capital actively *into* defense / energy / national-security, *out of* civilian/consumer names lacking crisis resilience), not "buy the dip." Companies irrelevant in peacetime become critical in wartime.
- **Why:** The distinction is the trap: Fear-Dislocation and Crisis/Wartime *look* identical on the tape (everything red) but demand opposite moves — in one the thesis is unchanged so you add to your existing names; in the other the thesis-set itself rotated, so adding to peacetime names is buying into a structurally impaired cohort. Collapsing the two means you "buy the dip" on a name whose category just lost its forward revenue.
- **Picture:** A sudden conflict pops small caps but isn't a TAM increase — not a multi-x or a long hold *unless* the war structurally shifts the category (the TAM-changing vs flow-only distinction applied to a regime).

## CAPEX CASCADE — THE TRANSMISSION SPINE

### The cascade chain, in order
- **What:** Demand moves down the physical chain in a fixed order: **Hyperscaler CapEx → neocloud deals → semiconductors → memory → substrates/optics → raw materials.** Everything in the macro section reads off positions on this spine.
- **Why:** Naming the explicit order lets you locate any name on the chain and ask "what just moved one layer up, and has this layer priced it yet?" — the lag between an upstream signal and a downstream re-rate IS the tradeable gap. Without the ordered spine you treat each report as isolated news instead of a demand signal for everyone beneath it.
- **Picture:** A foundry blowout confirms downstream GPU/substrate/memory demand before those names move.
- **Tags:** kind=principle | gotcha=no | model_already_knows=no | placement=skill_body
- **Source:** legacy "CapEx Cascade" | correction=none

### Overflow pattern — and the tier-1-buys-on-open-market sold-out tell
- **What:** When hyperscaler capacity is full, demand overflows to alternative providers (neoclouds, specialized compute) — sudden revenue acceleration the market hasn't priced. The confirmation that supply has *nowhere left to go*: **the tier-1 leader itself buying the scarce sub-component on the open market** = *structurally* sold out, not cyclically tight. Displaced second-tier buyers then scramble to the next merchant, re-rating the small name. The buy is the un-allocated #2/#3 underneath, NOT the sold-out leader.
- **Why:** The default reflex reads "leader sold out" as bullish for the leader — but the leader is already priced for it; the un-priced move is the displaced demand cascading to the next merchant down. And the leader buying on the *open market* (vs internal allocation) is the discriminator between cyclical tightness (resolves with capex) and structural shortage (physics-bound) — it's revealed preference louder than any order-book number.
- **Picture:** Leader buying its own scarce input on the open market → structural sell-out → the small #2 transceiver/substrate merchant re-rates.

- **Source:** legacy "Overflow pattern" | correction=none

### Proxy read-through — one company's print is another's demand signal
- **What:** After any major report, ask: "which supply-chain names does this validate or invalidate?" A foundry blowout confirms downstream GPU/substrate/memory demand; a hyperscaler CapEx raise validates the neocloud and component tiers. Position *before* the read-through is priced across the chain — the reporting company's numbers are public; the implications are not yet.
- **Why:** This is the cascade's direct alpha: the market prices the reporting name's beat instantly but takes days-to-quarters to re-price the suppliers it structurally implies, because no analyst covers both the reporter and the three-hops-down node. The window between the public number and the priced implication is the edge.
- **Picture:** Hyperscaler CapEx raise → validates the neocloud tier and the component tier before those names move.

### Strategic-incentive floor — the threatened hyperscaler backstops its consumers
- **What:** When a hyperscaler's core demand is threatened by customer in-housing, it may backstop alternative consumers to defend its own revenue — an invisible price floor on those backstopped names. Map these flows.
- **Why:** This floor is invisible to standalone analysis (it lives in the *backer's* incentive, not the name's balance sheet), so it changes the downside without showing up in any of the name's own metrics — a larger entity whose position depends on a smaller one will defend it, dropping the real downside below what the screen says.
- **Picture:** 2069151544320016527 (NBIS) — NVDA's strategic incentive to prop up GPU-lenders is part of the de-risking, alongside Mag7 customers and the $4.1B raise.

### Sympathy selloff — split real counterparty damage from pure association
- **What:** When one name reports badly, peers drop too. Run the mechanism check across the peers and separate **real counterparty damage** (a customer/supplier whose forward revenue actually takes the hit) from **pure association** (sold off only for sharing a theme/sector — no revenue link). The association drop is mechanical and usually round-trips in 1–2 sessions; the counterparty drop may be real.
- **Why:** Both look identical on the tape (a peer down on a peer's bad print), but only one changes forward revenue — so the split is exactly the forward-revenue test applied to a selloff, and getting it wrong means either buying a name whose real customer just vanished, or passing on a free 1–2-session round-trip.
- **Picture:** 2006301094394335399 (MRVL) — "displacement" rumors physically impossible mid-cycle; the drop was sympathy/association, not real counterparty damage.

## CATALYST HIERARCHY (real vs fake)

### The governing test — "does this change forward revenue?"
- **What:** One test sits over every headline, drop, and catalyst: **does this change forward revenue?** Yes → reality moved, re-rate. No → only sentiment/mechanics moved → that's the opportunity (or noise).
- **Why:** This is the root axiom (R1) — the market's only durable mispricing is the gap between price and structure, and forward revenue is the single observable that separates a structural change from a sentiment/mechanical one. Every other catalyst rule is this test specialized to a case; carry the test and you can adjudicate a catalyst the lists below never named.
- **Picture:** An earnings *beat* alone is execution (forward revenue unchanged); a beat *with a guidance raise* is real (forward revenue moved up).
- **Tags:** kind=principle | gotcha=no | model_already_knows=no | placement=skill_body
- **Source:** legacy "Catalyst Hierarchy" / R1 | correction=none

### Real catalysts (move forward earnings) vs fake (noise / entry)
- **What:** REAL: index inclusion, mega-contract, policy/EO, an earnings beat *only with a guidance raise*, export-control-creates-monopoly (structural). FAKE (ignore, or use as entry): CFO resignation, conferences, government shutdown, tariff tweets. Two non-test handles: **dividend front-running** is a *timing* catalyst (optimize entry on already-validated large-caps), and **analyst initiations/upgrades lag — they follow price** (a V8/V10 read, never a reason).
- **Why:** The lists are just the forward-revenue test pre-computed for recurring cases, so you don't re-derive the obvious ones live. The two handles are the traps: a dividend catalyst is real but only as *timing* (not a thesis), and treating an analyst upgrade as a catalyst inverts cause and effect — the upgrade is the lagging confirmation that the move already happened.
- **Picture:** Government shutdown headline → fails the test → noise or entry; never a thesis driver.
- **Tags:** kind=principle | gotcha=no | model_already_knows=yes | placement=skill_body
- **Source:** legacy real/fake lists | correction=none

### Prediction-market gauge — >90% priced = the event is noise; a surprise is not
- **What:** When a market (e.g., Polymarket) prices an event >90%, it's already priced in — the actual occurrence is noise, and any retail panic on the news is a buying opportunity. A *surprise* outcome (low prior, it happened) is NOT priced — real repositioning required.
- **Why:** "Priced in" is usually a vibe; a prediction-market number makes it *checkable*, which is the whole point — it tells you mechanically whether the occurrence carries new information (low prior) or none (≥90% prior). Without the gauge you mistake a foregone conclusion's headline for a catalyst and chase a move that already happened.
- **Picture:** A >90% Polymarket event prints → retail panics on the "news" → that panic is the buy, the occurrence is noise.
- **Tags:** kind=principle | gotcha=no | model_already_knows=no | placement=skill_body
- **Source:** legacy "Prediction-market gauge" | correction=none

### TAM-changing vs flow-only — the third cell the real/fake split misses
- **What:** A catalyst can be *entirely real* (real event, real sector inflows) yet *not raise addressable revenue*. That's neither noise nor a re-rate — it's a **bounded, short-term** trade. A TAM-changing catalyst (durable new forward revenue) can be held like a structural bottleneck; an attention/flow-only one drives a catalyst *pop*, not a re-rate, and must not be held like one. Match the *horizon* to which kind it is.
- **Why:** The binary real/fake split silently buckets a flow-only catalyst as "real" and tempts you to hold a pop like a re-rate — bleeding the gain back when the flow rotates out. The third cell preserves the tacit nuance that "real" ≠ "durable": forward *revenue* changing is the hold license; mere inflows changing is only a horizon-bounded trade.
- **Picture:** A sudden conflict is a real tailwind but not a TAM increase — small caps may pop, but it isn't a multi-x or a long hold unless the war structurally shifts the category.
- **Tags:** kind=principle | gotcha=yes | model_already_knows=no | placement=skill_body
- **Source:** legacy "TAM-changing vs flow-only" | correction=none

### Regulation cuts both ways — read the clauses for mechanically-compelled buyers/sellers
- **What:** A new legal framework is often the *biggest* catalyst: it can re-rate a whole **category** (a stablecoin/payments law turning an issuer into quasi-infrastructure) or cap a model as a structural **headwind** (a DTC/clinical-access rule). Ask: one name or the whole category? tailwind or headwind? When you suspect the headwind side, *read the actual clauses* for who is **mechanically compelled** to buy or sell, and trade that against the consensus narrative when they conflict.
- **Why:** Regulation changes what a business is *allowed to be* — the most durable forward-revenue change there is — and the market trades the *framing* ("safety," "consumer protection") while the *clauses* mechanically force flows the narrative hides. A reserve/collateral mandate the largest holder can't meet with illiquid assets makes it a forced seller of its liquid ones; a clause banning a yield stream removes a structural inflow. The "safety/consumer-protection" framing often masks an incumbent-chokepoint motive. The mechanically-compelled flow is the truth; the narrative is the misdirection.
- **Picture:** A stablecoin reserve mandate → read the clause → the largest holder is a *forced seller* of liquid assets it must post as collateral; trade that against the bullish "regulatory clarity" narrative.
- **Tags:** kind=principle | gotcha=yes | model_already_knows=no | placement=skill_body
- **Source:** legacy "Regulation cuts both ways" | correction=none

### Short-squeeze setup — upside catalyst on a good name, trap on a broken one
- **What:** Extreme short interest (>30–40% of float) on a *profitable, growing* name is an **upside** catalyst — a violent feedback loop on any good news, not merely a risk. On a broken name it's a trap; a squeeze needs a real thesis underneath.
- **Why:** SI is symmetric in the generic finance sense, but the asymmetry the methodology adds is that a fundamentally-strong name with crowded shorts has the squeeze as a *bonus* catalyst layered on a thesis you'd own anyway, whereas a squeeze on a broken name is a pure mechanical pop with nothing underneath to hold — the fundamentals gate decides which one you have.
- **Picture:** >30–40% SI on a profitable, growing name → any guidance raise lights a feedback loop; the same SI on a no-revenue name is a falling-knife dressed as a squeeze.
- **Tags:** kind=principle | gotcha=no | model_already_knows=yes | placement=skill_body
- **Source:** legacy "Short-squeeze setup" | correction=none

### Competitor launch or fumble — apply the test to the category
- **What:** A rival shipping, delaying, or failing is a demand-share catalyst, not just news — apply the forward-revenue test to the *category*, not only the named company.
- **Why:** A competitor's fumble doesn't change the named company's numbers directly, but it redistributes the category's forward revenue toward the names that didn't fumble — so the catalyst is real for them precisely because you ran the test one level up at the category.
- **Picture:** A rival's launch delay → demand-share shifts to the shipping competitor → real forward-revenue catalyst for the latter.
- **Tags:** kind=principle | gotcha=no | model_already_knows=no | placement=skill_body
- **Source:** legacy "Competitor launch (or fumble)" | correction=none

### Earliest signal = the raw-material spot, not the equity
- **What:** Information propagates in a fixed order — supply-chain derivative signals (commodity spot, procurement, utilization) → paid industry reports → public news → repricing → earnings confirmation. Edge is proportional to how early you observe. A spot move with no equity move *yet* is the gap. Named sources: LME / Fastmarkets / Argus (metals), TrendForce / DRAMeXchange (memory), spot indices (specialty gases, PGMs).
- **Why:** Earnings confirmation is the *end* of the chain — by then the move is mostly done — so anchoring on the print structurally guarantees you're late. Watching the raw-material spot (which leads the equity by the propagation lag) is the only way to observe the signal before it's priced; the equity is the last domino, not the first.
- **Picture:** A NAND spot move on DRAMeXchange with the memory equities still flat = the forward leg the market hasn't priced.
- **Tags:** kind=principle | gotcha=no | model_already_knows=no | placement=skill_body
- **Source:** legacy "Earliest signal" / Discovery "track the signal" | correction=none

## MACRO → MICRO: TRANSMISSION PATHWAYS

### Classify every macro event: fundamental change (size to it) vs entry-only (trade the dip)
- **What:** Every macro event reaches a stock through a transmission pathway; identify which and you know whether it **changes fundamentals** (size to it) or is **entry-only** (trade the dip, don't re-rate the thesis). That classification IS the move. The six channels below are recurring, not closed — trace a novel event's path the same way and place it on the same fundamental-vs-entry line.
- **Why:** The error a macro headline invites is uniform: either over-react (re-rate a thesis on an entry-only dip) or under-react (treat a fundamental shift as mere noise). The fundamental-vs-entry axis is the single decision that prevents both, and it generalizes — so you carry the axis, not a memorized channel list.
- **Picture:** Export controls → monopoly premium = *changes fundamentals* (size). Tariff noise on a zero-exposure name = *entry only* (trade the dip).
- **Tags:** kind=principle | gotcha=no | model_already_knows=no | placement=skill_body
- **Source:** legacy "Macro → Micro: transmission pathways" | correction=none

### Rate cuts — route bidirectionally into the cohort, and READ the gauge
- **What:** A rate move hits both the CapEx cascade *and* a rate-sensitive cohort. Read the cut probability off a market-implied source (Polymarket / Fed futures) as the regime read, then route it *past* the cascade into the cohort it actually marks up: long-duration growth (far-out earnings marked UP — the sharpest re-rate), small-caps / Russell carrying floating-rate debt, and direct beneficiaries (housing, fintech lenders). Run it **bidirectionally** — when cuts get priced *out*, the same map inverts and the win rotates to banks / stablecoins / insurers earning the spread. Institutions front-run the cut, so position before it prints and treat the cut date itself as a likely sell-the-news dip to add into. *Changes fundamentals.*
- **Why:** Far-out revenue is the most rate-sensitive (DCF), so a cut marks up long-duration growth hardest — but the move only pays if you front-run it, because institutions price the cut in before the date and the print itself is a sell-the-news dip. The bidirectional discipline exists because a "no-cuts" regime is not the *absence* of a trade — it's a *different* trade (spread-earners), and forgetting that leaves half the rate map unused.
- **Picture:** 2069151544320016527 (NBIS) — a 3× rate cut marks up far-out earnings, one channel of the multi-channel macro→micro stack on the thesis.
- **Tags:** kind=principle | gotcha=no | model_already_knows=no | placement=skill_body
- **Source:** legacy pathway #1 / DB 1969151544320016527 | correction=none

### Rate regime: never vibe it, never delete it (two hard stops)
- **What:** READ the gauge — pull the actual market-implied probability (Polymarket / Fed futures) before you route or invert. When the question already states a regime, honor it unless a sourced gauge contradicts it. Two hard stops: **(1)** to DISMISS a stated rate regime you must quote an actual gauge number — "0 cuts priced" with no Polymarket/Fed-futures figure shown is vibing, not sourcing, and does not earn the dismissal; absent a real number, honor the stated regime and run the routing. **(2)** Never DELETE the rate angle — the move completes in one direction or the other (route into the long-duration cohort when cuts are priced in, *or* rotate to spread-earners when priced out). A terminated move — "rates don't matter here" — is the failure, not an answer.
- **Why:** This is a loss-hardened guardrail against two specific model-native failures: asserting a rate regime from a macro *feeling* (which fabricates the entire downstream routing on no evidence), and silently dropping the rate angle when it's inconvenient (which leaves the highest-leverage macro channel unanalyzed). Both are catastrophic because the rate read sets the cohort the whole answer marks up or down — a vibed or deleted gauge corrupts everything routed off it. Requiring a quoted number to *dismiss* a regime is what stops a confident-sounding "0 cuts" from overriding a real one.
- **Picture:** Question states "in a 3-cut regime" → you must either honor it and route into long-duration growth, or quote a Fed-futures number that contradicts it — you may not reply "rates don't matter for this name."
- **Tags:** kind=rule | gotcha=yes | model_already_knows=no | placement=skill_body
- **Source:** legacy pathway #1 "Input discipline" | correction=none

### The other five transmission channels (each pre-classified)
- **What:** **#2 Export controls → monopoly premium** — the Western/domestic alternative inherits pricing power, the removed competitor can't re-enter (*structural — changes fundamentals*). **#3 Tariff noise → TACO trade** — no real transmission on names with zero actual exposure (*entry only*). **#4 Algo earnings misparse → non-fundamental selling** — algos misread a one-time charge distorting EPS; reverses once humans process it (*entry only*). **#5 Tax harvesting → quality oversold** — artificial Nov–Dec selling, January mean-reversion (*entry only*). **#6 Credit stress → weak fail, strong survive** — share shifts to strong balance sheets (*changes fundamentals*, a feature for survivors).
- **Why:** These are the forward-revenue test pre-applied to the recurring macro channels, so you classify fast — but the value is the split they encode: #2 and #6 are the only two that move fundamentals (size to them), while #3/#4/#5 are pure mechanical dips (trade, don't re-rate). Confusing a #6 (a survivor *gaining* durable share) for an entry-only dip under-sizes a real structural win.
- **Picture:** Credit stress → a strong-balance-sheet survivor *gains* market share as the weak fail — a fundamental tailwind, not a dip to trade and exit.

## GEOPOLITICS & POLICY (US-investor lens)

### Never bet against US policy
- **What:** National-security verticals (AI, space, energy, critical minerals, quantum) are government-backed — policy provides a durable floor pure market forces can't replicate. Most geopolitical headlines are noise; the policy-backed verticals are the few that are structural.
- **Why:** A government backstop is a forward-revenue floor that doesn't clear when sentiment does — it's spending mandated by national-security priority, not by the market — so fading it means shorting an entity with effectively unlimited balance sheet and a non-economic objective function. The floor changes the downside of every name in the vertical.
- **Picture:** A national-security AI/space/minerals name has a policy floor under it that a pure-market peer in a non-strategic sector lacks.
- **Tags:** kind=principle | gotcha=no | model_already_knows=no | placement=skill_body
- **Source:** legacy "Never bet against US policy" | correction=none

### Reshoring = Made-in-America moat (first-class for a US-only book)
- **What:** When export controls / security mandates force reshoring, the **domestic** producer in an industry whose competitors sit in high-risk geographies gains an instant structural moat. For a US-only book this is a first-class thesis source — the policy tailwind and the US listing align.
- **Why:** Reshoring converts a policy mandate directly into a US-listed name's forward revenue *and* a defensible moat (the foreign competitor is now legally/strategically excluded), so it's the rare thesis where the structural edge and the accessibility constraint point the same way — no resolution ladder needed.
- **Picture:** 2029219794025644382 (AAOI) — the only pure US transceiver fab; the made-in-America moat is part of the 3x call.
- **Tags:** kind=principle | gotcha=no | model_already_knows=no | placement=skill_body
- **Source:** legacy "Reshoring" / DB 2029219794025644382 | correction=none

### Export controls create monopolies — but invert it inside the geography (moat vs hostage)
- **What:** Export controls hand the direct US-listed beneficiary a monopoly premium (check whether it's already priced) — but this is the *outside-the-geography* case. A US name that *manufactures inside* the controlled region is a **hostage** to it, not a beneficiary: the controlling state holds an export/permit lever over the name's own output. A US-listed materials maker manufacturing inside the controlled region is often **both at once** — net the two, don't read the concentration one-directionally.
- **Why:** The same geographic concentration is a moat or a hostage depending on *who holds the lever* — the name (prices the scarce node) or the state (can permit-block the name's output). Reading it one-directionally is the trap: you either miss the monopoly premium on the outside beneficiary, or you credit a "beneficiary" that the controlling state can switch off at will. Both errors come from not asking which side of the permit wall the name sits on.
- **Picture:** A US-listed rare-earth/gallium maker manufacturing *inside* the controlling country is beneficiary of the scarcity AND trapped behind its government's permit wall — net the two.
- **Tags:** kind=principle | gotcha=yes | model_already_knows=no | placement=skill_body
- **Source:** legacy "Export controls create monopolies" / Winner-Gates moat-vs-hostage | correction=none

### A subsidy/grant is a three-way signal, not just cash
- **What:** Beyond the funding floor, grant *inclusion* is outsourced supply-chain diligence — a state only funds genuine chokepoints, so inclusion validates the node is critical (two governments independently funding the same node compounds it). And a name surfacing in a published policy blueprint / supply-chain impact-analysis is a *datable* forward catalyst that reprices on a multi-month lag.
- **Why:** Most read a grant as a one-time funding event and stop — but it carries two more signals: it's a sovereign's diligence that the node is a real chokepoint (a third-party validation you couldn't otherwise buy), and the published blueprint is a *dated, lagged* catalyst that reprices months later as the policy machinery turns. Treating it as cash-only leaves the validation and the lagged catalyst on the table.
- **Picture:** A government industrial-policy blueprint naming a chain's controllers is a free, pre-vetted, often-ranked list with explicit control percentages no screen hands you (Discovery's "government impact-analysis mining").
- **Tags:** kind=principle | gotcha=no | model_already_knows=no | placement=skill_body
- **Source:** legacy "A subsidy/grant is a three-way signal" | correction=none

### Cross-fire casualty / TACO — verify genuinely-zero exposure first
- **What:** A broad geopolitical selloff or an overreaction to a known/empty tariff threat drags down quality names with *zero* exposure to the trigger — a gifted dip, fundamentals unchanged (V1). Always verify genuinely-zero exposure before buying.
- **Why:** This is Fear-Dislocation produced by a policy headline — the asymmetry is real *only if* exposure is genuinely zero, which is the verification step that separates a gifted dip from a name actually in the cross-fire. Skipping the verification is how a "TACO dip" turns into catching a name whose forward revenue really did take the hit.
- **Picture:** A tariff tweet drags a 60%-margin oligopoly with no actual tariff exposure down with the sector — verify the zero exposure, then it's the buy.
- **Tags:** kind=principle | gotcha=yes | model_already_knows=no | placement=skill_body
- **Source:** legacy "Cross-fire casualty / TACO" | correction=none

### Read the policy persona → rotation map
- **What:** Whoever holds power (administration, central bank) has a consistent set of favored and disfavored verticals (energy vs climate, defense vs aid, crypto-permissive vs -hostile). Map that persona to a structural tailwind/headwind and rotate the *hunt* accordingly — policy is a multi-quarter driver, not a headline.
- **Why:** A policy persona is the most *predictable* macro input — it telegraphs which verticals get a multi-quarter tailwind before any specific headline prints — so reading it as a standing rotation map lets you position the *hunt* ahead of the individual catalysts it will produce, rather than reacting to each one.

The ladder turns a fuzzy "things feel risky" into a graded, actionable response.

### Post-liquidation entry — wait for exhaustion, then quality snaps back first

## HIDDEN SINGLE-POINT-OF-FAILURE

The shared node is simultaneously the alpha (it *is* the bottleneck) and the tail risk, so naming it makes the real, undiversified risk visible behind the diversified-looking list.

## CONTRARIAN TIMING

### Position before the catalyst — sentiment direction is the inverse signal
- **What:** News is data; sentiment *direction* is the inverse signal. Institutions front-run known catalysts, so position *before* the event, not on the announcement. When multiple *independent* contrarian indicators converge on a fundamentally strong name, maximum pessimism is the asymmetric entry.
- **Why:** The edge is buying when others sell — but only on a fundamentally strong name, because converging *independent* pessimism on such a name is the market mispricing structure via sentiment (the R1 gap), whereas the same pessimism on a weak name is correct. Positioning on the announcement rather than before guarantees you buy *after* the front-runners, into the sell-the-news.
- **Picture:** Multiple independent contrarian indicators converging on a fundamentally strong name at maximum pessimism → the asymmetric entry, positioned before the catalyst prints.
- **Tags:** kind=principle | gotcha=no | model_already_knows=no | placement=skill_body
- **Source:** legacy "Contrarian timing" | correction=none

### Narrative-flood-as-accumulation-tell — fire the prove-the-math fade
- **What:** A sudden *coordinated flood* of bearish notes on a name you hold — a cluster of sell-side "bubble" calls riding the macro fear-theme-of-the-week (Oil / LNG / Helium / Iran / "KOSPI crash") — is itself the accumulation tell, and it often fires *before* any price drop. An unusual flood of negative news usually means a large player needs liquidity, or wants retail to paper-hand the float so they can take it. Separate the forward-earnings reality (contracts, price hikes already printing) from the ambient narrative, and **fade the narrative with hard margin math** when the fundamentals are intact and improving.
- **Why:** A *coordinated* bearish flood with no forward-revenue change behind it is information about *flow* (someone wants the float), not about the company — and it often *precedes* the price drop, which is the tell that it's manufactured rather than reactive. The prove-the-math fade (DB's single most-repeated quantitative pattern) is what converts "the narrative feels wrong" into a checkable verdict: if the math says margins aren't actually threatened, the gap between the doom narrative and the unchanged numbers IS the trade.

### YOUR bearishness only on a real forward-revenue change — never on noise volume
- **What:** The discriminator that stops the narrative-fade from rubber-stamping every dip: when *you* turn bearish it must be on a real forward-revenue change — a dilution-quality flip, a designed-out cut — **never on the volume of the noise**. A loud doom-theme is not, by itself, a bear signal *or* a buy signal; only forward revenue changing is.
- **Why:** The narrative-flood tell is dangerously easy to over-apply — if "everyone's bearish, so I fade it" were unconditional, you'd buy every dip including the ones where the thesis genuinely broke. The guardrail forces your *own* bearishness onto the same forward-revenue test you apply to everyone else's, so noise volume can never author a sell (or a buy) — only a structural change can. This is what keeps "think independently" from collapsing into "always do the opposite of the crowd."
- **Picture:** A dilution-quality flip (an at-market ATM replacing a contract-funded build) is a real bear trigger; a louder cluster of "bubble" tweets is not.
