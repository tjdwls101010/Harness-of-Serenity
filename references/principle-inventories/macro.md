## REGIME → AGGRESSION

### Regime is the aggression dial, not a destination
- **What:** Read the pipeline's `regime` + `risk_level` into a *posture* — how aggressive the hunt, how loud the go. Risk-On → lean aggressive on conviction; Transition → raise the bar. The moment a question is macro *and more*, run the regime read first and let every downstream name inherit the setting. Don't re-derive the gauges the pipeline computes — interpret them.
- **Why:** The same name is a buy in one regime and a pass in another; the regime is the multiplier on every conviction call, so resolving it first prevents you from sizing a thesis correctly in isolation but wrong for the tape. Treating macro as a separate answerable question (and stopping there) wastes the dial's real job — conditioning everything after it.
- **Picture:** Type A "장 어때" enters at the regime call and may stop there; "관세 때문에 뭐 사" is macro AND supply-chain AND discovery — walk the union in dependency order, regime first.

### The four regime pillars — hyperscaler CapEx direction is THE load-bearing one
- **What:** Four pillars decide the regime — ① hyperscaler CapEx direction, ② net liquidity (= Fed balance sheet − TGA − RRP, pipeline-computed; read directional, not a point estimate), ③ credit conditions, ④ VIX structure (contango vs backwardation). For any AI-adjacent book **CapEx direction is load-bearing**: increasing = tailwind, flat/declining = reassess everything downstream. Combine the pillars — elevated VIX *alone* is insufficient.
- **Why:** The entire CapEx cascade (semis → memory → substrates → raw materials) is downstream of hyperscaler spend, so its direction is the single signal that validates or invalidates the largest cluster of theses at once — it changes forward revenue for the whole chain, not sentiment. Reading VIX in isolation gives a fear spike with no fundamental read; you'd buy a dip the cascade is actually turning against. (Net liquidity is named with its three components so you can attribute a swing to a TGA refill vs an RRP drain rather than treating "liquidity" as an opaque mood — but it comes from code: interpret, don't recompute.)

### Fear-Dislocation is the best buying environment — but wait for forced-seller exhaustion first
- **What:** Fear elevated but fundamentals intact → the single *best* buying environment (V1); the supply-chain thesis is unchanged, the price is gifted. This is where the fear-dip discrimination (mechanism check, prove-the-math, IO-into-the-drop, kill-signal clear) earns its keep. Operationally: after forced selling (margin calls, redemptions) exhausts, quality snaps back *first* — wait for the exhaustion, *then* enter.
- **Why:** The market reacts to *sentiment faster than to structure*, so Fear-Dislocation is the maximal gap between price and unchanged structure — by construction where the asymmetry is fattest. Naming it as the *best* environment (not merely "a" buyable one) is the behavioral nudge that keeps you buying when the tape feels worst, exactly when the instinct is to wait. But forced selling is price-insensitive and overshoots, so entering *before* it exhausts is catching the knife mid-cascade; the discipline is to let the mechanical seller finish — quality recovers first because it's what the remaining real buyers want.
- **Picture:** Post-liquidation snap-back: a margin-call cascade → wait for forced sellers to exhaust → quality reverts first (the V1 mechanism made concrete).

### Crisis/Wartime is a 4th regime — structural rotation, NOT buy-the-dip
- **What:** When active conflict, a sustained geopolitical crisis, or a structural policy shift (sanctions, trade-war escalation) creates a *durable* sector rotation — not a temporary fear spike — treat it as a distinct regime the pipeline won't flag. The action is "rotate the portfolio" (capital actively *into* defense / energy / national-security, *out of* civilian/consumer names lacking crisis resilience), not "buy the dip." Companies irrelevant in peacetime become critical in wartime.
- **Why:** The distinction is the trap: Fear-Dislocation and Crisis/Wartime *look* identical on the tape (everything red) but demand opposite moves — in one the thesis is unchanged so you add to your existing names; in the other the thesis-set itself rotated, so adding to peacetime names is buying into a structurally impaired cohort. Collapsing the two means you "buy the dip" on a name whose category just lost its forward revenue.
- **Picture:** A sudden conflict pops small caps but isn't a TAM increase — not a multi-x or a long hold *unless* the war structurally shifts the category (the TAM-changing vs flow-only distinction applied to a regime).

## CAPEX CASCADE — THE TRANSMISSION SPINE

### The cascade chain, in order — and the proxy read-through it generates
- **What:** Demand moves down the physical chain in a fixed order: **Hyperscaler CapEx → neocloud deals → semiconductors → memory → substrates/optics → raw materials.** Everything in the macro section reads off positions on this spine. Direct corollary (the cascade's alpha): after any major report, the reporting company's numbers are public but the supply-chain *implications* are not yet — locate the names one-to-three hops down and position *before* the read-through is priced.
- **Why:** Naming the explicit order lets you locate any name and ask "what just moved one layer up, and has this layer priced it yet?" — the lag between an upstream signal and a downstream re-rate IS the tradeable gap. The market prices the reporting name's beat instantly but takes days-to-quarters to re-price the suppliers it structurally implies, because no analyst covers both the reporter and the three-hops-down node. Without the ordered spine you treat each report as isolated news instead of a demand signal for everyone beneath it.
- **Picture:** A foundry blowout confirms downstream GPU/substrate/memory demand before those names move; a hyperscaler CapEx raise validates the neocloud tier and the component tier before those names move.
- **Tags:** kind=principle | gotcha=no | model_already_knows=no | placement=skill_body
- **Source:** legacy "CapEx Cascade" + "Proxy read-through" (consolidated) | correction=none

### Overflow pattern — and the tier-1-buys-on-open-market sold-out tell
- **What:** When hyperscaler capacity is full, demand overflows to alternative providers (neoclouds, specialized compute) — sudden revenue acceleration the market hasn't priced. The confirmation that supply has *nowhere left to go*: **the tier-1 leader itself buying the scarce sub-component on the open market** = *structurally* sold out, not cyclically tight. Displaced second-tier buyers then scramble to the next merchant, re-rating the small name. The buy is the un-allocated #2/#3 underneath, NOT the sold-out leader.
- **Why:** The default reflex reads "leader sold out" as bullish for the leader — but the leader is already priced for it; the un-priced move is the displaced demand cascading to the next merchant down. And the leader buying on the *open market* (vs internal allocation) is the discriminator between cyclical tightness (resolves with capex) and structural shortage (physics-bound) — it's revealed preference louder than any order-book number.
- **Picture:** Leader buying its own scarce input on the open market → structural sell-out → the small #2 transceiver/substrate merchant re-rates.

- **Source:** legacy "Overflow pattern" | correction=none

### Strategic-incentive floor — map the cross-cohort backstop flow
- **What:** When a hyperscaler's core demand is threatened by customer in-housing, it may backstop alternative consumers to defend its own revenue — an invisible price floor on the backstopped names. At the *macro* level, map these flows as a regime-level floor across the cohort: when a threatened larger entity defends a consumer tier, the whole tier's downside is propped, not just one name. (The per-name moat-plane version of this — "who structurally NEEDS this company" — is owned by serenity-analysis's 3-D moat graph; do not re-derive it here.)
- **Why:** This floor is invisible to standalone analysis (it lives in the *backer's* incentive, not the name's balance sheet), so it changes the cohort's downside without showing up in any single name's metrics — a larger entity whose position depends on a smaller one will defend it, dropping the real downside below what the screen says. Read as a *flow* it tells you which whole tier carries a hidden floor in the current regime.
- **Picture:** A threatened hyperscaler backstopping its GPU-lender tier props the downside of every name in that tier, not just the one you're looking at — map the flow, then let analysis value the individual name's moat plane.
- **Tags:** kind=principle | gotcha=no | model_already_knows=no | placement=skill_body
- **Source:** legacy "Strategic-incentive floor" | correction=none

### Sympathy selloff — split real counterparty damage from pure association
- **What:** When one name reports badly, peers drop too. Run the mechanism check across the peers and separate **real counterparty damage** (a customer/supplier whose forward revenue actually takes the hit) from **pure association** (sold off only for sharing a theme/sector — no revenue link). The association drop is mechanical and usually round-trips in 1–2 sessions; the counterparty drop may be real. (When the split is run as the 4-step on a *single* name you'd own, that lives in serenity-analysis's falling-knife discrimination, with the full MRVL worked example.)
- **Why:** Both look identical on the tape (a peer down on a peer's bad print), but only one changes forward revenue — so the split is exactly the forward-revenue test applied to a selloff, and getting it wrong means either buying a name whose real customer just vanished, or passing on a free 1–2-session round-trip.
- **Picture:** 2006301094394335399 (MRVL) — "displacement" rumors physically impossible mid-cycle; the drop was sympathy/association, not real counterparty damage. (Worked in full in analysis 4-step; cited here.)

## CATALYST HIERARCHY (real vs fake)

### The governing test — "does this change forward revenue?" (R1 specialized to catalysts)
- **What:** One test sits over every headline, drop, and catalyst: **does this change forward revenue?** Yes → reality moved, re-rate. No → only sentiment/mechanics moved → that's the opportunity (or noise). Every catalyst rule below is this test pre-computed for a recurring case; carry the test and you can adjudicate a catalyst the lists never named.
- **Why:** R1 is always loaded (CLAUDE.md) — the market's only durable mispricing is the gap between price and structure, and forward revenue is the single observable separating a structural change from a sentiment/mechanical one. This item only specializes it: the catalyst hierarchy is R1 applied to events, so lead straight into the catalyst cases rather than re-teaching the root axiom.
- **Picture:** An earnings *beat* alone is execution (forward revenue unchanged); a beat *with a guidance raise* is real (forward revenue moved up).

### Two catalyst handles the forward-revenue test alone won't catch
- **What:** The real/fake enumeration (index inclusion / mega-contract / EO / guidance-raised beat / export-control monopoly = REAL; CFO resignation / conference / shutdown / tariff-tweet = noise) is just R1 pre-applied — don't spend words re-sorting the obvious. Keep only the two non-obvious handles: **dividend front-running is a *timing* catalyst** (optimize entry on already-validated large-caps — never a thesis), and **analyst initiations/upgrades LAG price** (a V8/V10 read, never a reason).
- **Why:** A competent analyst re-derives the real/fake list from R1 the moment R1 is stated, so spending a block on it is the distrust-tax. The two handles are the traps the test misses: a dividend catalyst is real but only as *timing*, and treating an upgrade as a catalyst inverts cause and effect — the upgrade is the lagging confirmation that the move already happened.
- **Picture:** A government-shutdown headline fails the test (noise/entry, never a driver); an analyst upgrade *after* a 20% run is confirmation of the move, not a reason to chase it.

### Prediction-market gauge — >90% priced = the event is noise; a surprise is not
- **What:** When a market (e.g., Polymarket) prices an event >90%, it's already priced in — the actual occurrence is noise, and any retail panic on the news is a buying opportunity. A *surprise* outcome (low prior, it happened) is NOT priced — real repositioning required.
- **Why:** "Priced in" is usually a vibe; a prediction-market number makes it *checkable*, which is the whole point — it tells you mechanically whether the occurrence carries new information (low prior) or none (≥90% prior). Without the gauge you mistake a foregone conclusion's headline for a catalyst and chase a move that already happened.
- **Picture:** A >90% Polymarket event prints → retail panics on the "news" → that panic is the buy, the occurrence is noise.
- **Tags:** kind=principle | gotcha=no | model_already_knows=no | placement=skill_body
- **Source:** legacy "Prediction-market gauge" | correction=none

### TAM-changing vs flow-only — the third cell the real/fake split misses
- **What:** A catalyst can be *entirely real* (real event, real sector inflows) yet *not raise addressable revenue*. That's neither noise nor a re-rate — it's a **bounded, short-term** trade. A TAM-changing catalyst (durable new forward revenue) can be held like a structural bottleneck; an attention/flow-only one drives a catalyst *pop*, not a re-rate, and must not be held like one. Match the *horizon* to which kind it is.
- **Why:** The binary real/fake split silently buckets a flow-only catalyst as "real" and tempts you to hold a pop like a re-rate — bleeding the gain back when the flow rotates out. The third cell preserves the tacit nuance that "real" ≠ "durable": forward *revenue* changing is the hold license; mere inflows changing is only a horizon-bounded trade.
- **Picture:** A sudden conflict is a real tailwind but not a TAM increase — small caps may pop, but it isn't a multi-x or a long hold unless the war structurally shifts the category.
- **Tags:** kind=principle | gotcha=yes | model_already_knows=no | placement=skill_body
- **Source:** legacy "TAM-changing vs flow-only" | correction=none

### Regulation cuts both ways — read the clauses for mechanically-compelled buyers/sellers
- **What:** A new legal framework is often the *biggest* catalyst: it can re-rate a whole **category** (a stablecoin/payments law turning an issuer into quasi-infrastructure) or cap a model as a structural **headwind** (a DTC/clinical-access rule). Ask: one name or the whole category? tailwind or headwind? When you suspect the headwind side, *read the actual clauses* for who is **mechanically compelled** to buy or sell, and trade that against the consensus narrative when they conflict.
- **Why:** Regulation changes what a business is *allowed to be* — the most durable forward-revenue change there is — and the market trades the *framing* ("safety," "consumer protection") while the *clauses* mechanically force flows the narrative hides. A reserve/collateral mandate the largest holder can't meet with illiquid assets makes it a forced seller of its liquid ones; a clause banning a yield stream removes a structural inflow. The "safety/consumer-protection" framing often masks an incumbent-chokepoint motive. The mechanically-compelled flow is the truth; the narrative is the misdirection.
- **Picture:** A stablecoin reserve mandate → read the clause → the largest holder is a *forced seller* of liquid assets it must post as collateral; trade that against the bullish "regulatory clarity" narrative.

- **Source:** legacy "Regulation cuts both ways" | correction=none

### Short-squeeze setup — the threshold and the "bonus on a name you'd own anyway" frame
- **What:** Extreme short interest (**>30–40% of float**) on a *profitable, growing* name makes a squeeze a **bonus upside catalyst layered on a thesis you'd own anyway**; the fundamentals gate decides which case you have — the same SI on a broken name is a trap, a pure mechanical pop with nothing underneath to hold.
- **Why:** SI is symmetric in the generic sense, so don't re-teach squeeze mechanics; the serenity edge is the threshold + the framing. A fundamentally-strong name with crowded shorts gives the squeeze for free on top of a thesis already justified on its own merits, whereas a squeeze on a broken name is a falling-knife dressed as a squeeze — the gate, not the SI number, tells them apart.
- **Picture:** >30–40% SI on a profitable, growing name → any guidance raise lights a feedback loop; the same SI on a no-revenue name is a falling-knife dressed as a squeeze.

- **Why:** A competitor's fumble doesn't change the named company's numbers directly, but it redistributes the category's forward revenue toward the names that didn't fumble — the test only finds the catalyst when you run it one hop up. (One sentence is the whole item; the model derives the rest from R1.)
- **Picture:** A rival's launch delay → demand-share shifts to the shipping competitor → real forward-revenue catalyst for the latter.

### Earliest signal = the raw-material spot, not the equity
- **What:** Information propagates in a fixed order — supply-chain derivative signals (commodity spot, procurement, utilization) → paid industry reports → public news → repricing → earnings confirmation. Edge is proportional to how early you observe. A spot move with no equity move *yet* is the gap. Named sources: LME / Fastmarkets / Argus (metals), TrendForce / DRAMeXchange (memory), spot indices (specialty gases, PGMs). (serenity-discovery *applies* this to time an entry on a found name; the propagation chain + named sources are owned here.)
- **Why:** Earnings confirmation is the *end* of the chain — by then the move is mostly done — so anchoring on the print structurally guarantees you're late. Watching the raw-material spot (which leads the equity by the propagation lag) is the only way to observe the signal before it's priced; the equity is the last domino, not the first.
- **Picture:** A NAND spot move on DRAMeXchange with the memory equities still flat = the forward leg the market hasn't priced.

## MACRO → MICRO: TRANSMISSION PATHWAYS

### Classify every macro event: fundamental change (size to it) vs entry-only (trade the dip)
- **What:** Every macro event reaches a stock through a transmission pathway; identify which and you know whether it **changes fundamentals** (size to it) or is **entry-only** (trade the dip, don't re-rate). That classification IS the move, and it generalizes — trace a novel event's path the same way and place it on the same line.
- **Why:** The error a macro headline invites is uniform — over-react (re-rate on an entry-only dip) or under-react (treat a fundamental shift as noise). The fundamental-vs-entry axis is the single decision that prevents both. (Stated in one sentence; the model owns the axis — the value is the pre-classified channels below, not re-teaching the axis.)
- **Picture:** Export controls → monopoly premium = *changes fundamentals* (size). Tariff noise on a zero-exposure name = *entry only* (trade the dip).
- **Tags:** kind=principle | gotcha=no | model_already_knows=no | placement=skill_body
- **Source:** legacy "Macro → Micro: transmission pathways" (framing compressed) | correction=none

### Rate cuts — route bidirectionally into the cohort, and READ the gauge
- **What:** A rate move hits both the CapEx cascade *and* a rate-sensitive cohort. Read the cut probability off a market-implied source (Polymarket / Fed futures) as the regime read, then route it *past* the cascade into the cohort it actually marks up: long-duration growth (far-out earnings marked UP — the sharpest re-rate), small-caps / Russell carrying floating-rate debt, and direct beneficiaries (housing, fintech lenders). Run it **bidirectionally** — when cuts get priced *out*, the same map inverts and the win rotates to banks / stablecoins / insurers earning the spread. Institutions front-run the cut, so position before it prints and treat the cut date itself as a likely sell-the-news dip to add into. *Changes fundamentals.*
- **Why:** Far-out revenue is the most rate-sensitive (DCF), so a cut marks up long-duration growth hardest — but the move only pays if you front-run it, because institutions price the cut in before the date and the print itself is a sell-the-news dip. The bidirectional discipline exists because a "no-cuts" regime is not the *absence* of a trade — it's a *different* trade (spread-earners), and forgetting that leaves half the rate map unused.
- **Picture:** 2069151544320016527 (NBIS) — a 3× rate cut marks up far-out earnings, one channel of the multi-channel macro→micro stack on the thesis.
- **Tags:** kind=principle | gotcha=no | model_already_knows=no | placement=skill_body
- **Source:** legacy pathway #1 / DB 1969151544320016527 | correction=none

### Rate regime: never vibe it, never delete it (two hard stops)
- **What:** READ the gauge — pull the actual market-implied probability (Polymarket / Fed futures) before you route or invert. When the question already states a regime, honor it unless a sourced gauge contradicts it. Two hard stops: **(1)** to DISMISS a stated rate regime you must quote an actual gauge number — "0 cuts priced" with no Polymarket/Fed-futures figure shown is vibing, not sourcing, and does not earn the dismissal; absent a real number, honor the stated regime and run the routing. **(2)** Never DELETE the rate angle — the move completes in one direction or the other (route into the long-duration cohort when cuts are priced in, *or* rotate to spread-earners when priced out). A terminated move — "rates don't matter here" — is the failure, not an answer.
- **Why:** A loss-hardened guardrail against two model-native failures: asserting a rate regime from a macro *feeling* (which fabricates the entire downstream routing on no evidence), and silently dropping the rate angle when inconvenient (which leaves the highest-leverage macro channel unanalyzed). Both are catastrophic because the rate read sets the cohort the whole answer marks up or down — a vibed or deleted gauge corrupts everything routed off it. Requiring a quoted number to *dismiss* a regime is what stops a confident-sounding "0 cuts" from overriding a real one. (This is the macro-specific *application* of CLAUDE.md's number-discipline non-negotiable — it stays skill-local because the bidirectional-routing and honor-the-stated-regime behavior is unique to the rate move.)
- **Picture:** Question states "in a 3-cut regime" → you must either honor it and route into long-duration growth, or quote a Fed-futures number that contradicts it — you may not reply "rates don't matter for this name."

## GEOPOLITICS & POLICY (US-investor lens)

### Never bet against US policy
- **What:** National-security verticals (AI, space, energy, critical minerals, quantum) are government-backed — policy provides a durable floor pure market forces can't replicate. Most geopolitical headlines are noise; the policy-backed verticals are the few that are structural.
- **Why:** A government backstop is a forward-revenue floor that doesn't clear when sentiment does — it's spending mandated by national-security priority, not by the market — so fading it means shorting an entity with effectively unlimited balance sheet and a non-economic objective function. The floor changes the downside of every name in the vertical.
- **Picture:** A national-security AI/space/minerals name has a policy floor under it that a pure-market peer in a non-strategic sector lacks.
- **Tags:** kind=principle | gotcha=no | model_already_knows=no | placement=skill_body
- **Source:** legacy "Never bet against US policy" | correction=none

### Reshoring = Made-in-America moat (first-class for a US-only book)
- **What:** When export controls / security mandates force reshoring, the **domestic** producer in an industry whose competitors sit in high-risk geographies gains an instant structural moat. For a US-only book this is a first-class thesis source — the policy tailwind and the US listing align.
- **Why:** Reshoring converts a policy mandate directly into a US-listed name's forward revenue *and* a defensible moat (the foreign competitor is now legally/strategically excluded), so it's the rare thesis where the structural edge and the accessibility constraint point the same way — no resolution ladder needed.
- **Picture:** A CHIPS-Act / security-mandate reshoring order forces domestic sourcing of a component whose only alternative suppliers sit in a high-risk geography → the sole US-listed domestic producer inherits a policy-mandated moat. (AAOI is the worked transceiver-fab case — owned by discovery/analysis; illustrated here policy-first to avoid over-loading one ticker across docs.)
- **Tags:** kind=principle | gotcha=no | model_already_knows=no | placement=skill_body
- **Source:** legacy "Reshoring" | correction=none

### Export controls create monopolies — the outside-the-geography monopoly-premium catalyst
- **What:** Export controls hand the direct US-listed beneficiary *outside* the controlled geography a **monopoly premium** — the removed competitor can't re-enter, so the survivor inherits pricing power. Check whether it's already priced before treating it as a catalyst. (The inversion — a US name that *manufactures inside* the controlled region is a permit-hostage, not a beneficiary, and a maker who does both must net the two — is a per-name moat-evidence read owned by serenity-analysis's Geographic moat plane; route there, don't read concentration one-directionally here.)
- **Why:** Export controls remove a competitor permanently (legal exclusion, not a price the rival can undercut), so the survivor's pricing power is durable forward revenue, not a cyclical spike — that's why it's a structural *catalyst*. The "check if priced" step matters because a well-telegraphed control is often already in the survivor's multiple; the un-priced version is the trade.
- **Picture:** An export ban removes the foreign supplier of a scarce node → the US-listed alternative inherits a monopoly premium; verify it isn't already in the multiple, then it's the catalyst.

## LIQUIDITY & SEASONALITY

### Four drains — count convergence on a ladder
- **What:** Four *independent* channels pull risk capital out: crypto precursor shock · Fed/policy hawkish surprise · AI credit stress · carry-trade unwind. Count convergence: **one = noise; two = tighten stops on marginal positions; three+ = cut leverage, hold only highest conviction** (systemic withdrawal, not idiosyncratic noise).
- **Why:** Each drain alone is survivable noise, but because the channels are *independent*, multiple firing at once means risk capital is leaving through several doors simultaneously — that's a systemic-withdrawal signature no single channel can produce, and the convergence count is what distinguishes it from one-off idiosyncratic stress. The ladder turns a fuzzy "things feel risky" into a graded, actionable response.
- **Picture:** Crypto shock + a hawkish Fed surprise + AI credit stress firing together (3) → cut leverage, hold only highest conviction.

### Nov–Dec → January — the most actionable seasonal (build then harvest)
- **What:** Tax-loss harvesting forces selling in quality names irrespective of fundamentals in Nov–Dec (the exact fear-dislocation this methodology exploits), with mean-reversion in January → build in Nov–Dec, harvest in January. ## CONTRARIAN TIMING

### Position before the catalyst — sentiment direction is the inverse signal
- **What:** News is data; sentiment *direction* is the inverse signal. Institutions front-run known catalysts, so position *before* the event, not on the announcement. When multiple *independent* contrarian indicators converge on a fundamentally strong name, maximum pessimism is the asymmetric entry.
- **Why:** The edge is buying when others sell — but only on a fundamentally strong name, because converging *independent* pessimism on such a name is the market mispricing structure via sentiment (the R1 gap), whereas the same pessimism on a weak name is correct. Positioning on the announcement rather than before guarantees you buy *after* the front-runners, into the sell-the-news.
- **Picture:** Multiple independent contrarian indicators converging on a fundamentally strong name at maximum pessimism → the asymmetric entry, positioned before the catalyst prints.
- **Tags:** kind=principle | gotcha=no | model_already_knows=no | placement=skill_body
- **Source:** legacy "Contrarian timing" | correction=none

### Narrative-flood-as-accumulation-tell — fire the prove-the-math fade
- **What:** A sudden *coordinated flood* of bearish notes on a name you hold — a cluster of sell-side "bubble" calls riding the macro fear-theme-of-the-week (Oil / LNG / Helium / Iran / "KOSPI crash") — is itself the accumulation tell, and it often fires *before* any price drop. An unusual flood of negative news usually means a large player needs liquidity, or wants retail to paper-hand the float so they can take it. Separate the forward-earnings reality (contracts, price hikes already printing) from the ambient narrative, and **fade the narrative with hard margin math** when the fundamentals are intact and improving.
- **Why:** A *coordinated* bearish flood with no forward-revenue change behind it is information about *flow* (someone wants the float), not about the company — and it often *precedes* the price drop, which is the tell that it's manufactured rather than reactive. The prove-the-math fade (DB's single most-repeated quantitative pattern) is what converts "the narrative feels wrong" into a checkable verdict: if the math says margins aren't actually threatened, the gap between the doom narrative and the unchanged numbers IS the trade.

### YOUR bearishness only on a real forward-revenue change — never on noise volume
- **What:** The discriminator that stops the narrative-fade from rubber-stamping every dip: when *you* turn bearish it must be on a real forward-revenue change — a dilution-quality flip, a designed-out cut — **never on the volume of the noise**. A loud doom-theme is not, by itself, a bear signal *or* a buy signal; only forward revenue changing is.
- **Why:** The narrative-flood tell is dangerously easy to over-apply — if "everyone's bearish, so I fade it" were unconditional, you'd buy every dip including the ones where the thesis genuinely broke. The guardrail forces your *own* bearishness onto the same forward-revenue test you apply to everyone else's, so noise volume can never author a sell (or a buy) — only a structural change can. This is what keeps "think independently" from collapsing into "always do the opposite of the crowd."
