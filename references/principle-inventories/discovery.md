### Discovery-Escalation trigger — when a chain-trace becomes a discovery (the quantified gate)
- **What:** Discovery does not fire on every name. It fires when mapping a target's chain reveals a high-growth chain whose key input is **concentrated (top-3 supplier share > 70%)** in a supplier with **market cap < 1/10 of the target.** That pair of thresholds is the operating gate: a concentrated *and* radically-smaller node down-chain is the leveraged mispricing the discovery toolkit exists to surface. When it trips, stop analyzing the target and run the discovery moves on the down-chain node. (CLAUDE.md carries this as a *routing* cue; here it is the *operating* threshold.)
- **Why:** Without a numeric trigger discovery either never fires (you keep re-analyzing the visible target) or fires on every tangential supplier (free association — see the recursive-hop tests). The two thresholds isolate exactly the asymmetry that pays: concentration > 70% means the node is a genuine chokepoint (not a fragmented commodity), and MC < 1/10 of the target means the chokepoint's repricing has nowhere near caught up to the demand it gates — the smaller the node relative to the demand leaning on it, the more violent the re-rate when attention arrives.
- **Picture:** Legacy Analysis Protocol step 4 — a high-growth target whose substrate input is ~60-70% controlled by a sub-$1B supplier (AXTI/InP, DB 2004936335702753729: $700M node feeding a multi-trillion AI buildout) trips both thresholds and escalates from "analyze the target" to "discover the node."

### The two-axis discovery frame (vertical + horizontal)
- **What:** Discovery has exactly two finding-axes, and most misses come from running only the first. Vertical: trace the money flow *down* a chain to the deepest input nobody prices. Horizontal: transfer *across* from a name that already won to its not-yet-re-rated sibling one generation out. Goal of both: surface the smallest-MC, least-covered node the chain structurally depends on, then hand that ticker to `analyze`. The pipeline analyzes a ticker; it cannot *find* one — discovery is pure agent work (WebSearch + SEC reading + tracing).
- **Why:** The default failure is re-analyzing the incumbent everyone already sees — the end-node that coverage and price already saturate (R3: attention pools on the large, visible, already-priced node and thins down-chain and out to the un-re-rated analog). Naming both axes explicitly forces you off the saturated node: if you've only traced down, you've skipped the cheaper sibling; if you've only transferred across, you've skipped the deeper input. The alpha is definitionally where attention can't reach, so a frame that lists where attention *does* reach is the map of where to *not* look.
- **Picture:** DB 2029219794025644382 (AAOI) — horizontal transfer: LITE already won transceivers ($55B MC, priced); the return lived in AAOI ($7.1B), the only pure US transceiver fab one architecture-step out sharing the role.

### Trace the chain down — the vertical spine
- **What:** Follow money flow *down* from the end-product: end-product → integrator/OEM → major components → sub-components → raw materials → equipment → feedstock/chemicals. At each layer ask the layer questions: how many suppliers? lead time for new capacity? geographic concentration? % of end-product cost? substitutes? The deepest layers carry the thinnest coverage and the most mispricing.
- **Why:** Analysts covering the end-product rarely look past the major components, so mispricing compounds with depth — coverage is a function of proximity to the visible end-node, not of where value structurally concentrates. The five layer-questions are not a checklist for its own sake; each is a direct probe of *concentration* (the thing that makes a node a chokepoint) and *substitutability* (the thing that makes the chokepoint durable). Skip the questions and you mistake a fragmented commodity layer for a bottleneck.
- **Picture:** DB 2004936335702753729 (AXTI) — traced the Western AI roadmap down past "substrate" to InP feedstock, where the $700M AXT + Sumitomo duopoly (~60-70%) sits with demand ~2× supply.

### The recursive hop and its three tests
- **What:** When you find a bottleneck, ask "what does *this* company depend on?" and trace one hop further — but each hop must EARN the next by passing all three tests or you stop: (1) **Does concentration rise?** Recompute supplier count / geographic share one layer down — if *more* concentrated you're moving toward the chokepoint; if *more* fragmented you've already passed it. (2) **Does the end-use demand a higher grade?** The deeper input often splits into a commodity tier and a thin high-spec tier only the end-use can accept — a distinct, far tighter monopoly. (3) **Is the dependency captive?** A supplier that *looks* vertically integrated but still single-sources one outside input is captive to that source — unmask it and the "integrated" name is really a pass-through to the deeper bottleneck. **Stop hopping when concentration stops rising or a real substitute appears** — that layer is the bottleneck.
- **Why:** Without the three tests the recursive hop degenerates into free association — you can always name *a* supplier-of-the-supplier, infinitely, and end up on an irrelevant commodity. The tests are a gradient-ascent stopping rule: concentration-rising is the signal you're climbing toward the chokepoint, and its reversal is the signal you've crested it. The purity/spec gate is the most transferable test (quartz, photoresist, medical-grade polymers, aerospace alloys all hide a tighter monopoly in their high-spec cut), because "the commodity is abundant" is exactly the reasoning that makes the market miss the thin qualified tier.
- **Picture:** Legacy worked example — a ~$700M substrate the whole AI buildout leans on is cut from a feedstock, but only a laser-grade 6N-purity variant qualifies (~80% one country's output); research stops at "substrate," the +500% lived one hop deeper in the purity tier. (DB 2004936335702753729, AXTI/InP.)

### Reconstruct the link nobody disclosed (the un-connected A→C trap)
- **What:** The highest-alpha link is usually the one no filing names. A 10-K's competitor list and supplier disclosures surface only the large-caps a US filer *chose* to name — never the small-cap two hops down whose design win is the actual thesis. Rebuild the confidential link from four sources: (1) **the small-cap's own transcripts** — it names its design wins and end-customers even when the customer won't name it; (2) **end-customer keynote → spec-match** — a hyperscaler/OEM discloses a part's specs at a product launch; match those specs to the only supplier that can hit them; (3) **warrant / strategic-investment filings** — an agreement's implied volumes, or an equity stake taken at diligence, reveal a supply contract the income statement hasn't printed yet; (4) **paid market-share / channel reports** — third-party share data names suppliers no SEC document does. The trap all four defeat: **A→B is public and B→C is public, but nobody draws A→C.** This is OSINT, not lookup, and transfers wholesale (a payments rail, an energy interconnect, a defense BOM each hide their real dependency the same way). (The defense/national-security specialization of this move lives in the reference item below.)
- **Why:** Disclosure is asymmetric by design — the big filer names its peers and major suppliers for risk-factor and competition reasons, but the small supplier two hops down is precisely the party with no disclosure obligation and every incentive to stay quiet about a marquee customer. So the link that matters most is structurally the one you must *reconstruct*, not retrieve. The A→C trap is why the market leaves it unpriced: each leg is individually visible and individually boring; only the synthesis is alpha, and synthesis is exactly what a separated-data-point market doesn't do (R1).
- **Picture:** DB 2029219794025644382 (AAOI) — "3-4 hyperscalers buy all capacity" reconstructed from the small-cap's own disclosure, not the hyperscalers'. Also the confidential-BOM reconstruction archetype (MSScorps / SpaceX-style BOM: pin the unnamed supplier by spec-matching the end-customer's published part specs).

### Transfer from a proven winner — the 5-step decomposition ("the next $X")
- **What:** The single most frequent discovery move is *"the next $X"* — and done right it is NOT "find a cheaper lookalike." It is a five-step decomposition: (1) Strip the proven winner to its **structural role**, not its product — "the EML-laser bottleneck for *current-gen* optics," not "a laser company." (2) Name the **architecture shift** that spawns a *next-gen* version of that role — a new interconnect standard, a settlement-rail change, a packaging shift — and the role it creates. (3) Name the supplier filling that role **per orthogonal slice**: current-gen vs next-gen, customer A vs B, scale-up vs scale-out — each slice is a candidate. (4) **Rank the cohort by how re-rated each already is** and isolate the laggard — the one the ecosystem's move hasn't reached. (5) **Test whether the lag is exploitable**: a coverage gap, sub-$1B MC, a foreign OTC line, or ticker/segment-naming confusion = exploitable; broken fundamentals = the lag is *deserved*, skip it.
- **Why:** "Cheaper lookalike" buys you a value trap — a name is cheap-relative-to-the-winner either because attention hasn't reached it (exploitable) or because its fundamentals are broken (deserved), and only step 5 separates the two. The incumbent is already priced, so re-analyzing it is wasted motion (R3); the return lives in the sibling one architecture-step out that *shares the structural role*. Decomposing by role rather than product is what makes the move sector-blind — a payments rail re-rating under a regulatory shift, or the clear #2 in a launch market, decomposes identically. (This is the generative twin of thesis-inheritance: inheritance *validates* the pattern on a name you already hold; this *generates* the name.)
- **Picture:** DB 2029219794025644382 (AAOI from LITE): role = "pure US transceiver fab"; architecture shift = next-gen optics ramp; laggard isolated = AAOI at $7.1B vs LITE $55B; exploitability = sub-scale MC with leverage to the same hyperscaler demand. Also DB 2027318568728273305 (IQE from a LandMark comp) — same transfer move on MOCVD reactor capacity.

### Rank a discovery cohort by de-risk, and consolidate into the winner
- **What:** When the transfer hands you a cohort rather than one name, rank for *capital*, not curiosity: order by how **de-risked** each is relative to the proven leader — NOT by which is cheapest — and time entry to the leader's move (an analog stays buyable while the leader has *already* re-rated but the laggard's gap to it persists for coverage reasons). As execution data arrives and one name pulls ahead, **consolidate** — sell the laggards *into* the winner rather than nursing the basket. Use `discover` to compare the generated cohort side-by-side before committing (see the comparator item below).
- **Why:** Cheapest-first inverts the risk you're being paid for — the cheapest cohort member is often cheapest because it's least de-risked, so ranking by price systematically buys the worst odds. Ranking by de-risk-relative-to-leader respects that a transfer thesis is a *hypothesis* until execution confirms it, and consolidation prevents the slow bleed of a diversified basket whose alpha was always going to concentrate in one name. The timing window is real and structural: the laggard's gap persists for *coverage* reasons (attention lag), not fundamental ones, so it stays buyable after the leader has moved.
- **Picture:** Legacy — "sell the laggards into the winner rather than nursing the basket"; conviction doctrine note that a new core must clear a higher bar across a cohort. (Cohort-rank archetype mirrors DB 2033763395032519112's cycle-stage allocation logic.)

### The `discover` comparator — what it hands back, and what it does NOT
- **What:** `discover TICKER1 TICKER2 …` runs the pipeline's triage layer on each name and hands back a **side-by-side comparator** — a per-name triage read (health · momentum · catalyst · valuation substrate) for ranking a cohort against each other. It is a *comparator, not a grade and not a verdict*: a high triage read on a no-moat hot name, or a low one on a real early winner, is exactly the gap YOU resolve — the comparison surfaces the spread for judgment, it does not make the call. Run the cohort through `discover` first to isolate the laggard, then run the chosen name through `analyze`.
- **Why:** The whole reason discovery hands a *comparator* rather than a ranked verdict is the project's core boundary: code surfaces decision-substrate, the embodied analyst makes the decision. A composite triage number is a relative-ordering aid across a cohort, never an absolute judgment on one name — treating it as a verdict re-introduces the hidden grade the harness is built to remove, and a no-moat name screens "good" on momentum exactly when you most need your own gates. The comparator is most useful precisely where it *disagrees* with your read: that disagreement is the gap to resolve, not the answer.
- **Picture:** Legacy Data Contract — "`objective_screen` is a screen, not a verdict … a high screen on a no-moat hot name, or a low screen on a real early winner, is exactly the gap you resolve." Cohort use: feed the 5-step transfer cohort (e.g. AAOI vs IQE vs the LITE-analog set) to `discover`, isolate the laggard, hand it to `analyze`.

### Tacit technique — SEC competitor-list mining
- **What:** Read a known winner's 10-K competitor list as a pre-vetted *discovery* candidate pool — smaller, earlier names in the same chain, vetted by the filer's own counsel as genuinely competitive. Caveat: a *named* competitor is by definition visible enough to be named, so the list is a starting pool, not the endpoint — the confidential reconstructed link (above) is the higher prize.
- **Why:** Almost no one reads a 10-K for the filer's *rivals* — they read it for the filer — so the competitor list is free, pre-vetted diligence on the chain that sits unused as a discovery tool.
- **Picture:** Legacy — a leading optics maker's filed competitor list, read years early, held several names that later 20×'d.

### Tacit technique — the analyst-report gap (read for the omission)
- **What:** Read institutional reports for the *omission* — the supplier clearly in the chain but NOT on the list. "Institutions haven't found it yet" is exactly where the highest returns are; the names *on* the list are already priced.
- **Why:** This inverts how reports are normally consumed — the value isn't in what the analyst covered (already priced into every name on the page) but in the chain-position the analyst left blank. The omission is a direct readout of where attention has *not* reached, which (R3) is where mispricing lives. A competent reader stops at the named names; the edge is treating the gap as the signal.

### Tacit technique — the re-rating anomaly
- **What:** A whole ecosystem up hundreds of percent but one *critical* node still flat/small = the undiscovered gem. Ask *why* it hasn't moved — usually a coverage gap, not a flaw.
- **Why:** A critical node that didn't re-rate while its ecosystem did is a pure attention-lag signal, but the "ask why" guard is essential — a flat node can be flat because it's broken (deserved) or because nobody's looking (exploitable), and conflating the two is how you buy a falling knife dressed as a gem. The anomaly *generates* the candidate; the why-test *qualifies* it.
- **Picture:** Legacy §Discovery Doctrine re-rating anomaly. Live instance: DB 2027318568728273305 (IQE) — owns 100+ MOCVD reactors, ecosystem re-rated but IQE still priced for bankruptcy on £45M debt; the "why" = coverage gap + balance-sheet fear, not a capacity flaw.

### Tacit technique — second-order beneficiary
- **What:** From a catalyst, trace who supplies the supplier that just maxed out. A demand blowout at tier N forces tier N+1 from afterthought to bottleneck.
- **Why:** The market prices the obvious beneficiary (the company that reported) and stops; the supplier-of-the-supplier is a separate data point nobody connects (R1), so its forward revenue moves before its price does. The move is mechanical: when a node maxes out capacity, its own inputs become the new constraint — demand has nowhere to go but down one more layer.
- **Picture:** Legacy worked example — a transceiver maker's demand blowout → the epi-reactor makers it must now buy from go from afterthought to bottleneck.

### Tacit technique — convergence-find
- **What:** List the obvious players in a theme, then ask "which single company do *most* of these rely on?" That common dependency is often the real chokepoint. (Doubles as the hidden single-point-of-failure check — the same shared node is both the alpha and the tail risk; macro owns the bear-case/basket-risk reading of the same fact.)
- **Why:** A node that multiple visible players all depend on is, almost by construction, a chokepoint — yet the market prices each player separately and never aggregates the shared dependency into the single node's value. The same aggregation that finds the alpha also exposes the danger: a "diversified" set of names that all lean on one node is secretly one bet, so the convergence-find must be read in both directions.
- **Picture:** Legacy §Discovery Doctrine convergence-find. Archetype: a single-island foundry cluster or one country's refining that a whole basket of US-listed names quietly shares.

### Track the signal once you've found the node (application pointer)

### Discovery discipline — passing the gates is the bar, not "sounding good"
- **What:** You'll go through *tens* of candidates and reject most. Sounding "good" (a real chokepoint) is NOT the bar; passing the Winner Gates is. Don't fall in love with a name because you found it.
- **Why:** Discovery's generative moves are seductive — finding a clean chokepoint feels like the work is done, which is exactly the moment you stop applying the brutal power-law bar (V6) and start rationalizing. A chokepoint that can't monetize, can't price, or won't survive to the ramp is a fascination, not an investment; founder's-attachment to a self-discovered name is the specific bias that lets a sounds-good candidate skip the gates.

### Label every link CONFIRMED or DEDUCED — never let a DEDUCED node carry CONFIRMED conviction
- **What:** Tag every link CONFIRMED or DEDUCED. The reconstruction and transfer moves are powerful *because* they infer links no filing states — which is exactly why they can be wrong. A physics/spec deduction ("only this material meets that spec, so they must buy it") is a *lead*, not a thesis: tag it DEDUCED and size NOTHING on it until a filing, press release, or conference confirms it. Aggressive deduction *generates* candidates; confirmation *earns* conviction — never let a DEDUCED node carry a CONFIRMED node's conviction.
- **Why:** R3's un-seenness cuts both ways — the deduction has no external check, so its error mode is silent. Written in real misses: a supplier deduced from materials physics was the wrong vendor once the customer named its actual one at a trade show; an "unnamed leading customer" pinned to a hyperscaler simply never was. This is an *invariant* because sizing real capital on an unconfirmed inference is a catastrophic, irreversible error — it converts a free option (a lead) into a leveraged bet on a guess.
- **Picture:** Legacy §Discovery Doctrine + §Loss-Hardened Gotchas — "a supplier deduced from materials physics... was the wrong vendor once the customer named its actual one at a trade show." DB 2027318568728273305 (IQE) carries an explicit DEDUCED tag on the restructuring/turnaround bet.

### US-listed resolution ladder — never stop at "inaccessible"
- **What:** Discovery keeps surfacing foreign winners; the answer is never just "inaccessible, move on." Walk the ladder IN ORDER: (1) the name's **own US line first** — a US OTC ticker or unsponsored ADR of the *exact* company (highest fidelity, not a diluted basket); (2) else the **most-concentrated US ETF**, chosen by liquidity *and* expense — and name an options vehicle (deep, liquid chain) separately from a low-cost shares vehicle when they differ; (3) else the **nearest US-listed analog** in the same hop; (4) else **leave it as a map node** — name the foreign winner honestly, then route the capital to the accessible chokepoint *downstream* of it. Run whatever the ladder yields through `analyze`; use `discover` to compare a generated cohort first. Never silently bury the truth that the best pure-play is foreign. (This is discovery's canonical home for the ladder; CLAUDE.md keeps only the imperative, macro points here.)
- **Why:** The reflex "foreign → inaccessible → drop it" throws away capturable returns — a foreign micro-cap's own US OTC line tracks the local listing tick-for-tick (the +1900% you'd have skipped by stopping early). The ladder is ordered by *fidelity*: the exact company's own line is a pure-play, an ETF is a diluted basket, an analog is a proxy, and a downstream node is one hop removed — so you descend only when the higher rung genuinely doesn't exist. Naming the foreign winner honestly even at rung 4 keeps the analysis truthful (V7) and preserves the map for when a US line later appears.
- **Picture:** Legacy §US-Listed Resolution + §Discovery ladder — "a foreign micro-cap's own US OTC line tracks the local listing — the +1900% you'd have skipped." DB EWY (2030667380855083222) is the rung-2 instance: SK Hynix/Samsung accessed via the most-concentrated US ETF.

### The up-listing catalyst — depressed-trailing float handed to forward-priced buyers
- **What:** A foreign small-cap **up-listing onto a US exchange** (crossing the ~$1B MC / index-mandate thresholds) is a forced-buying catalyst: float shifts from local-retail to global-institutional, and the re-rate is *directional*, not just a lag. Local markets price off *trailing* revenue; US institutions price ~12mo *forward* — so the listing hands a depressed trailing-priced float to forward-priced buyers. A hostile local-media piece during the up-list window is the shake-out handing over that float, NOT a bear signal. (This is discovery's home for the up-listing mechanic — it's an accessibility/discovery event, not a regime read; macro points here.)
- **Why:** Two mechanisms compound: (a) index-mandate inclusion forces passive buying regardless of price, and (b) the buyer base's valuation horizon *changes* — the same cash flows get re-priced on a forward basis they were never priced on locally. That directional re-rate is invisible to anyone treating the up-listing as a mere liquidity event. The hostile-media inversion is the loss-hardened part: the natural read of bad press during a transition is "exit," but a coordinated bearish flood is often the accumulation tell (someone wants retail to paper-hand the float before the institutional bid arrives).
- **Picture:** Legacy §US-Listed Resolution up-listing + §Macro contrarian-timing narrative-flood — "a hostile local-media piece during the up-list window is the shake-out handing over that float, not a bear signal."

### ETF = thematic vehicle, analyzed via constituents (NAV composition is the discovery output)
- **What:** For an ETF, company-level bottleneck/margin analysis is meaningless — treat it as a *thematic vehicle* and analyze the underlying via its US-listed constituents. The discovery-specific output is **NAV composition**: what % of the ETF's NAV is actually the name you want, because a thematic ETF can be only fractionally the exposure you're after. This is rung-2 of the resolution ladder made precise. (The vehicle *mechanics* — IV tier → compressed-IV/Vega LEAP, where an OTM LEAP can double on Vega expansion alone — live in analysis "IV picks the vehicle"; discovery picks the *thematic exposure*, analysis picks the *instrument*.)
- **Why:** The ETF isn't a company, so the only discovery question is "does this basket actually hold the node I found, and at what weight?" — ignoring NAV composition risks buying a basket that's only fractionally the name you wanted. Whether to then express it as shares or a Vega-LEAP is an instrument decision that depends on the IV tier, which is analysis's territory; conflating the two led the legacy to teach IV mechanics inside a discovery clause.
- **Picture:** DB 2030667380855083222 (EWY) — "SK Square ~90% NAV is SK Hynix": the NAV-composition read that makes EWY a usable proxy for the SK Hynix node. (The Vega-LEAP expression of EWY belongs to analysis.)

### Sum-of-parts / hidden-stake unlock — the discovery trigger
- **What:** A name whose value is unlocked by a **subsidiary stake worth more than the parent's entire market cap** — or a hidden holding the market hasn't aggregated — is a discovery candidate. An activist (e.g. Palliser) or a credible SOP write-up surfacing the stake is itself the datable catalyst. That is the trigger; this is the convergence-find inverted (instead of the node many names share, you find the value one name *hides*). (The valuation mechanic — value the stake standalone, blend, the gap to parent MC is the unlock, with the WUS/NBIS worked evidence — is owned by analysis SECTION-2.)
- **Why:** The market prices a parent off its operating P&L and routinely ignores a non-core stake buried on the balance sheet or in a subsidiary's separate listing — a separated-data-point failure (R1) at the corporate-structure level. The discovery edge is *spotting that a name has such a stake at all* and that an activist/write-up has put a clock on it; quantifying the gap is the next-stage analysis step.
- **Picture:** DB 2067100222778704207 (WUS / Kunshan stake surfaced via Palliser) and DB 2007071108831338685 (NBIS Clickhouse) — both as *triggers* a discovery run would flag; the standalone valuation lives in analysis.
